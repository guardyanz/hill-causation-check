# Hill Causation Scorecard

**Claim:** Smoking → lung cancer

**Effect size:** Lung-cancer death rate among male smokers about 24 times that of non-smokers (+2,300%); about 8 times for women (+700%); heavy smokers RR ~10–20 versus non-smokers.

**Study design:** Prospective cohort studies plus case-control studies.

**Population and setting:** British doctors (Doll & Hill) and other adult populations in the UK and USA, followed for years after exposure was recorded.

**Source:** Hill, "The Environment and Disease: Association or Causation?", Proceedings of the Royal Society of Medicine 1965;58(5):295–300; Doll & Hill (1950, 1956).

## Scorecard

| # | Viewpoint | Verdict | Evidence (one line) | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Strength | Pass | Effect is very large: male smokers' lung-cancer death rate ~24× non-smokers (+2,300%), women ~8× (+700%), heavy smokers RR ~10–20. | Hill (1965); Doll & Hill (1950) |
| 2 | Consistency | Pass | Association replicated in dozens of studies, in different countries and populations, by different investigators. | Hill (1965); U.S. Surgeon General (1964) |
| 3 | Specificity | Weak | Smoking is linked to many diseases and lung cancer has other causes, so specificity is limited. | Hill (1965) |
| 4 | Temporality | Pass | Smoking precedes lung cancer by years in prospective cohorts; exposure was measured before the outcome. | Doll & Hill, British doctors study (1956) |
| 5 | Biological gradient (dose-response) | Pass | Risk rises with amount smoked: heavier smokers have higher lung-cancer rates than light smokers. | Hill (1965); Doll & Hill (1956) |
| 6 | Plausibility | Pass | Tobacco smoke contains known carcinogens that reach lung tissue. | Hill (1965) |
| 7 | Coherence | Pass | The rise in lung cancer follows the rise in cigarette smoking and fits the known biology of the disease. | Hill (1965) |
| 8 | Experiment | Pass | Risk falls after quitting smoking, and rises again with resumed heavy smoking. | Hill (1965); Doll & Hill (1956) |
| 9 | Analogy | Pass | Other inhaled carcinogens, for example occupational exposures, are accepted causes of lung cancer. | Hill (1965) |

## Verdict

**Tier:** STRONG

**Pass count:** 8 / 9

**What would change this verdict:** A large, well-conducted prospective study finding no association between smoking and lung cancer after full adjustment — no such study exists in the cited literature.

## Notes

- Temporality is the only absolute viewpoint: if X did not precede Y, the verdict is NOT ESTABLISHED, whatever the other scores.
- Specificity is the weakest viewpoint: failing it alone never sinks a verdict.
- This scorecard judges strength of evidence for a general claim. It does not give personal medical advice. Medical decisions belong with a qualified clinician.
